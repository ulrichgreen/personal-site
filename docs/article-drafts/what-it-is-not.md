# What It Is Not

> Every proposal is heard twice: once as what it is, once as what it threatens. Write the second hearing yourself, or your audience will write it for you.

**Description.** A short essay on a neglected genre: the "what this is not" section. A platform proposal that only describes itself leaves every stakeholder to infer the threat — the incumbent team hears "your work wasn't good enough," the app teams hear "mandate incoming," leadership hears "big commitment." Disarm each inference explicitly, before the ask: not a replacement, not a criticism of what exists, not a ban on the lower level, not finished or beyond criticism. Objections you name yourself cost a sentence; objections raised against you cost the meeting.

**Why it lands.** Engineers lose proposals they were right about because they defended the idea and ignored the room. This is a concrete, reusable move — practically a template — and it reads as craft rather than politics, which is what makes it acceptable to an engineering audience. The deeper point travels beyond pitching: negative space is part of any design's definition, and stating it is part of any design's documentation (the same discipline as writing down what a system refuses to do).

## Outline

1. **The second hearing.** You present an architecture; the room hears a reorganization. Threat perception, not technical merit, is where most internal proposals die — usually silently, in the meeting after the meeting.
2. **The inventory of threats.** For any shared-foundation pitch, the predictable four: the team that built the current thing (am I deprecated?), the teams asked to adopt (is this mandatory?), the maintainers of adjacent systems (does this eat my scope?), the budget holder (how big is this really?).
3. **Name each, then negate it.** Not a replacement of the underlying system — an extension of it into our stack. Not a claim that the existing work is inadequate. Not a ban on dropping to the lower level. Not finished, complete, or beyond criticism. Each sentence retires a meeting's worth of resistance.
4. **Scope the ask to match.** The request is not "approve this as done" but "support the direction and let it mature." An ask sized to the trust you've earned is an ask that can be granted.
5. **The general principle.** Definitions have negative space. A README that says what the tool does not do, a service that documents what it will never own, an API that names its non-goals — all of these are the same act of respect for the reader's inferences.
6. **Close.** The strongest version of your idea includes the strongest version of the fears it raises — answered, in your own words, before anyone has to ask.

**Notes.** From private direction notes (the platform pitch notes): pure rhetoric-craft, zero confidentiality risk. Short piece; could run as a pair with `a-timing-question.md` in a "pitching platform work" mini-series.
