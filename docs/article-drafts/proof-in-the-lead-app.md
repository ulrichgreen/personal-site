# Proof in the Lead App

> One application proves each piece of the foundation. Every other team adopts a de-risked follow — never a co-experiment. Direction states the destination; the schedule belongs to the adopters.

**Description.** How platform convergence actually works when you can't (and shouldn't) command other teams' roadmaps. Pick a lead application; prove each foundation piece there end to end — the auth pattern, the data layer, the pipeline — and let sibling apps converge axis by axis as each piece is proven. The bet commits the *destination* ("full convergence, no permanent second stack — every divergence is interim, not an alternative direction") while explicitly leaving cadence to the teams that own the apps. If an axis stalls, the direction doesn't change; only the timing does.

**Why it lands.** The two default convergence strategies both fail publicly: the mandate (platform team decrees, app teams comply resentfully with unproven pieces) and the federation (everyone experiments, nothing converges). The lead-app model is the third way, and its subtleties are the value: proving means *production*, not demo; "destination, not schedule" is what makes cross-team commitments honest instead of fictional; and the interim-vs-alternative distinction kills the quiet fork — a diverging stack is either on its way back or it's a decision someone needs to defend.

## Outline

1. **Two failed convergences.** The decree that shipped unproven patterns to five teams at once (five teams debugging the same novelty), and the federation that never converged (three auth models, "for now", forever).
2. **The lead app.** One production application walks each piece of the foundation first — through real traffic, real compliance, real incidents. Its scars are the documentation. Being lead is a role with costs, so name it deliberately.
3. **Proven means production.** A pattern is proven when it has survived the lead app's go-live gate, not its demo. The adopting team inherits an artifact with operational history, which is the only kind of de-risking that's real.
4. **Destination, not schedule.** The bet states where every app lands; it never states when. Cadence belongs to the teams that own the apps — because a commitment you make on someone else's behalf is a fiction with a date on it.
5. **Interim, not alternative.** The discipline that prevents quiet forks: every current divergence is explicitly one or the other. Interim means "converges when the piece is proven"; alternative means someone argues, in writing, for a permanent second stack. There is no unlabeled third state.
6. **When an axis stalls.** The direction holds; the timing moves. This asymmetry is the model's resilience — delay never gets misread as a decision, and nobody has to relitigate the destination to explain a slow quarter.
7. **Close.** You can't schedule other people's roadmaps. You can make the destination unambiguous and the path already-walked. That turns adoption from a leap into a step.
