# The Privacy Cost of Thinking With Machines

> My second brain is private. The machines that help me tend it are not. I haven't resolved this, and I distrust anyone who claims they have.

**Description.** An honest essay about an unresolved trade-off. A personal knowledge system — direction docs, half-formed bets, candid assessments — gains enormously from AI assistance: synthesis, distillation, research triage. But every assist routes the most sensitive thinking you own through third-party infrastructure: API terms you didn't negotiate, retention you can't verify, delivery channels with their own custody. The essay maps the actual decision surface (what categories of thought go through the pipe, what stays air-gapped, what the terms do and don't promise, where local models genuinely change the math and where they're security theater with worse synthesis) without pretending to a resolution the author doesn't have.

**Why it lands.** The discourse is bimodal — evangelists who never mention custody, and refuseniks who never admit what abstention costs — and almost nobody publishes from the honest middle: *I keep doing it, it keeps being worth it, and it keeps bothering me.* Readers running the same experiment get their unease named and structured: a taxonomy of sensitivity (mechanical notes vs. strategic assessments vs. things about other people — the last being where the calculus sharpens, because the data isn't only yours to spend), and the disciplines that actually reduce exposure versus the ones that just feel careful. Vulnerability plus rigor is the site's voice at its most distinctive.

## Outline

1. **The two inboxes.** The research digest lands on my phone each morning — genuinely useful, machine-made. To make it, a machine read my open questions, my bets, my doubts. Both facts, side by side, without flinching.
2. **What the pipe sees.** Not "data" — *thinking*: unfinished positions, candid evaluations, the questions you wouldn't ask aloud. Sensitivity taxonomies built for records fit badly; a half-formed doubt can be more revealing than any field marked PII.
3. **What the terms actually say.** Training exclusions, retention windows, subprocessors — read them once, honestly. Then note what no document can promise: that infrastructure never leaks, that policies never change, that acquisitions never happen. The residual is the price, and it doesn't round to zero.
4. **Other people's presence.** Meeting notes, colleagues' opinions, a team's internal tensions. The moment the corpus contains others, "my risk tolerance" stops being the operative question. This is the sharpest edge and the least discussed.
5. **Disciplines that count, rituals that don't.** Counts: category gates (what never enters the pipe), redaction at capture, local models for the most sensitive passes, delivery channels chosen for custody. Doesn't: vague anonymization that any cross-reference undoes; trusting the toggle because the toggle exists.
6. **The abstention ledger.** Refusing the machines has a cost too — slower synthesis, unread research, a direction layer that rots politely. Weigh it honestly or the argument is just aesthetics.
7. **Close.** No resolution — a standing review instead: a dated entry, revisited, of what goes through the pipe and why. The unresolved trade-off, treated like any other open question: on the record, owned, alive.

**Notes.** The most personal candidate in the set; publish only when comfortable — the essay's value *is* the candor. Keep all tooling and channel names generic. Related: `an-intelligence-agency-of-one.md`, `open-questions-are-live-forks.md` (the close reuses its discipline).
