# Teach the Machine to Hedge

> The most dangerous thing an assistant can do to your notes is make them sound finished. Uncertainty is content. Instruct for it.

**Description.** When you delegate synthesis — meeting summaries, research digests, document merges — to an AI, the default output failure isn't hallucination; it's *smoothing*. Disagreement becomes consensus, hedges become statements, the half-decided becomes the decided, because fluent prose is what the machine optimizes and fluency reads as certainty. This essay argues epistemic honesty must be explicitly instructed: record who disagreed and how; keep "probably" and "unless" attached to the claims that earned them; mark the partially-confirmed as such; read the whole source before writing a word of summary; and separate what was *said* from what it *implies*.

**Why it lands.** Everyone using AI for knowledge work has felt the unease of a summary that's too clean — and most haven't named the mechanism: the summary didn't add errors, it *removed doubt*, and removed doubt is how wrong decisions inherit false confidence downstream. The instructions are concrete and liftable (a checklist readers can paste into their own agent rules), and the essay's reach extends beyond AI: these were always the rules of honest summarization; the machine just industrialized the violation. A quietly contrarian companion to the "AI makes you faster" genre — speed of synthesis is worthless if the synthesis launders uncertainty.

## Outline

1. **The suspiciously clean summary.** The meeting was contentious; the summary reads like a press release. Nothing in it is false. Everything in it is *more decided* than the room was — and next quarter, the summary is the record.
2. **Smoothing, the failure mode with no error message.** Models are trained toward fluent, confident prose. Hedges, forks, and disagreements are friction in that objective — so they're the first thing lost, and the loss is invisible because the output reads better without them.
3. **The instructions that push back.** If speakers disagree or hedge, record it rather than smoothing it. Be explicit when something is uncertain or partially confirmed. Distinguish decisions from leanings from open questions. These go in the standing rules, not the per-task prompt — they're a posture, not a preference.
4. **Read everything first.** The summary written while reading is a running average; the summary written after reading can weigh the ending against the middle. Instruct full-pass reading before a word of synthesis — chunked if needed, skipped never.
5. **Said versus implied.** Demand both, labeled: what was actually stated, and what the writer infers as consequence. Merging them is how speculation gets a transcript's authority.
6. **Certainty is a property of the source.** The output's confidence must be inherited from the input, never manufactured in the summarizing. A synthesis that's more certain than its sources is fabricating — just at the epistemic layer instead of the factual one.
7. **Close.** You can delegate the reading. You can delegate the writing. What you cannot delegate is the standard for what counts as known — that one you have to write down.
