# Every Unowned Question Answers Itself

> A decision with no owner still gets made — by drift, by deadline, by whoever touched it last. The only thing ownership changes is whether anyone meant it.

**Description.** The recurring form of platform dysfunction isn't bad decisions; it's decisions nobody is positioned to make. The shared library whose sign-offs wait on a vacancy. The compliance obligation that's "live" while ownership is "unclear." The budget that became a dashboard because no one could approve or refuse a change to it. This essay makes the pattern visible with a construction worth stealing — *X without an owner is a lesser Y*: a budget without an owner is a dashboard, not a gate; direction without a decider is a recommendation; a temporary solution without an exit owner is permanent — and argues that assigning the question is a different, earlier act than answering it.

**Why it lands.** Engineers habitually treat ownership talk as management noise and then wonder why their technically-settled directions never land. The reframe — ownership as the thing that makes any other decision *real* — connects org mechanics to engineering outcomes in a way ICs actually feel: it explains why the excellent proposal died (no one owned saying yes) and why the bad state persists (no one owns saying no). The essay stays concrete: a walk through recognizable unowned questions and what each defaulted to.

## Outline

1. **The question that answered itself.** No one decided the flag registry's owner, so every team wrote flags its own way — which *was* the decision, made by nobody, owned by everybody's future.
2. **Unowned ≠ undecided.** Reality doesn't wait: traffic arrives, audits come due, dependencies upgrade. An unowned question is answered by circumstances, and circumstances optimize for nothing.
3. **The construction.** A budget without an owner is a dashboard, not a gate. Direction without a decider is a recommendation. A knowledge base without a governor drifts confidently wrong. A "temporary" bridge without an exit owner is architecture now. The pattern: subtract the owner and the noun degrades into its powerless cousin.
4. **Ownership is a prerequisite, not a formality.** Assigning "who can say yes, who can say no, who carries the duty" is a separate, earlier decision than the technical one — and it's the one that makes the technical one bind.
5. **The vacancy tax.** A platform with an ownership hole doesn't pause; it decays. Sign-offs queue, exceptions accumulate, and every consumer invents a local answer that will later cost a migration.
6. **A gentle instrument.** Keep a reverse index: concern → owner. A concern with no name beside it is a planning gap you can see — and "explicitly out of scope, here's why" is an acceptable name.
7. **Close.** You can defer a decision. You cannot defer its consequences. Owners are how a team chooses which decisions it means.

**Notes.** Synthesized from a signature construction that recurs across the planning corpus — the most "voice-native" candidate in the platform group. Keep all examples generic. Related: `on-legacy.md`, `a-gate-not-a-date.md` (gate items each carry an owner).
